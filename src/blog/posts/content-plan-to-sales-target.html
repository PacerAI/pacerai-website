Plan to sales target is the check that the bottom-up plan equals the number the company told the board it would hit. The sales target is the ending ARR, or the net new ARR, the operating plan is accountable for. The plan is the accounts, products and reps that are supposed to produce it. Pacer AI is a bottom-up sales forecasting and planning agent. This check is how a CRO makes plan with confidence. The real-time forecast tells you, later, whether you are still on the way.

What is a sales target, in this sense?

It is not a growth adjective. It is a dollar figure with a date and a definition. For a recurring-revenue company the clean version is ending ARR, tied to the same definition Finance uses in the ARR waterfall. If the board said “$120M of ending ARR,” the plan has to land on $120M when the lines are summed. If the board said “$20M of net new ARR,” the movements have to net to $20M. Pick one definition and keep it.

Why do the target and the plan come apart?

Four ordinary causes.

  1. The target was set top-down and the account work was never asked to add up to it. The gap is discovered in March.
  2. Two definitions of ARR. The CRM counts bookings. Finance counts recognized recurring revenue. Both figures are real, and they are not the target. The bridge between them is the waterfall.
  3. Quotas were issued on a different total. Sales is working a $22M number and the board is holding a $20M number, or the reverse. See plan to quota.
  4. The target moved and the plan did not. A hiring freeze, a slipped product, a lost renewal. The lines are stale. The slide is not.

The fix is the same in each case. Put the target and the sum of the lines in one view. If they differ, change the lines or change the target, and write down which one you did. That is the bottom-up plan.

How does the target relate to the in-year path?

Path to plan prorates the target to today and compares it with booked ARR. In the illustrative September 20 cut, plan to date is about $114.4M and booked ARR is $112.2M, so the company is $2.2M behind the target’s path. The forecast then asks whether weighted pipeline can close that gap by year end. The target does not move because the forecast moved. The forecast is a read. The target is a commitment.

Who is in the room when they disagree?

The CRO owns the commitment to the target. The CFO owns the definition and the reconciliation. RevOps owns the file that shows the sum. The VP of Sales owns the rep assignments. A meeting that “aligns” without changing a line or changing the target did not finish.

Board reporting is where this becomes public. The board-quality ARR snowball is the multi-period version of the bridge the target was built on. If the deck’s ending ARR is not the target you planned, the footnote should say why.

SaaS Capital, Bessemer’s Atlas and OpenView all publish the growth and retention ranges boards use as context. SaaStr is the operator’s version of the same conversation. Context is not a target. Your target is your book, summed.

What tie-out should the board be able to see?

One page, five figures, all on the same ARR definition.

  1. Opening ARR, signed by Finance.
  2. The planned movements: new, expansion, contraction, churn.
  3. Ending ARR, equal to the sales target.
  4. The sum of quotas, equal to the net of those movements.
  5. The exception list: dollars that depend on a hire, a product date, or a renewal you have already flagged.

In the illustrative book that page reads: open at $100M, add new logo and expansion, subtract what the plan expects to give back, close at $120M. The quota file sums to the same net $20M. If line 3 and line 4 differ by $1M, you do not have a target. You have a range, and ranges are what boards cut when a buyer asks a precise question.

Acquirers rebuild this page from your contracts. The Semrush and Adobe case is a public example of how a buyer reads quality of revenue, retention and the bridge, not the adjective in the plan. If your target’s bridge would not survive that reading, fix the bridge before the year, not during a process. The operating version of that reading is ARR waterfall reporting.

Keep the target stable inside the year. A CRO who resets the target every month to match the forecast has no path, because the ruler moves. Reset when the facts change and the board accepts a new commitment. Until then, report the variance. Variance is the point of having a target.

Write the target as ending ARR or as net new ARR, and do not switch units in the deck. A board that approved $120M of ending ARR and then sees a slide about “20% growth” cannot audit the plan. Percentages are a comment. The dollars are the target. If you report both, the dollars have to be the ones that sum from the lines, and the percentage has to be the result of those dollars, not a second commitment.

Frequently asked questions

What is plan to sales target?

It is the reconciliation of the bottom-up sales plan to the dollar figure the operating plan committed. The lines have to sum to the target, on Finance’s definition of ARR.

Is the sales target the same as the forecast?

No. The target is the commitment. The forecast is the current estimate of where the year lands. They are equal only when you are on the path and the pipe supports it.

What if the lines cannot reach the target?

Say so in the planning cycle. Add coverage, change the mix, or lower the target. Discovering it in the third quarter is a miss, not a planning method.
