How I Helped a Client Grow NRR from 101% to 105% in 6 Months

Pacer AI April 17, 2026

A PE-backed healthcare software company had reached the end of its hold period and was ready to exit. Their NRR was 101% — technically above the 100% threshold, but not the kind of number that commands a premium multiple. Six months later, it was 105%. Here’s how we got there.

What Did the Starting NRR Look Like?

The company’s ARR Snowball told a clear story. Beginning NRR at 100%, with -4% churn, -3% product churn, -1% downsell, +3% upsell, and +6% cross-sell, ending at 101%.

The cross-sell number looked healthy at 6%, but it was masking a problem. The company had acquired 5 businesses in 6 years under PE ownership, accumulating add-on products that customers didn’t want. Customers grew frustrated with add-on products, and that frustration was showing up as product churn — which was a leading indicator of full logo churn.

The ARR Snowball decomposition revealed that the growth wasn’t durable. Cross-sell was high because the company kept pushing add-ons, but product churn and logo churn were eating the gains.

2% Increase from Improving Upsell

The first lever was pricing discipline — two straightforward operational changes:

  • 1% from annual CPI price increases on renewals. The company had never implemented automatic price escalation. We added a standard CPI increase to the renewal process. This is table stakes for net revenue retention, but many PE-backed companies skip it, especially those that grew through acquisition rather than organic GTM maturity.
  • 1% from fixing revenue leakage. We found customers on outdated pricing — contracts that had auto-renewed for years without adjustment. Getting these customers onto current pricing recovered ARR that was already owed. SaaS Capital’s research on NRR consistently shows that pricing optimization is one of the fastest paths to NRR improvement.

Neither of these required new products, new customers, or new GTM motions. Just operational rigor applied to existing customer relationships.

Investigating Churn Drivers: The Product Churn Pattern

The ARR Snowball analysis uncovered a critical pattern: the majority of customers who churned had dropped a product (product churn) within twelve months before churning entirely.

This wasn’t random. The company had acquired 5 companies in 6 years, amassing add-on products that were difficult to manage. Customers’ frustration over add-on products they purchased led them to look at competitors’ offerings, which eventually led to switching to a competitor.

The Customer Data Cube made this pattern visible at the account-product level. Without account-product granularity, product churn and logo churn look like two separate problems. With it, the causal chain becomes clear: product frustration leads to product churn, which leads to logo churn.

2% Increase from Addressing Product Churn

The insight drove a counterintuitive recommendation: sunset products to grow NRR.

  • We recommended accelerating the sunsetting of 2 of the 4 add-on products immediately, rather than waiting for the end of the calendar year
  • We initiated the sunsetting of the other 2 add-on products to be removed next quarter, before sell-side fireside chats started

Less is more. By removing the products that were causing frustration, the company stopped the product churn → logo churn cascade. The short-term ARR hit from sunsetting was more than offset by the reduction in downstream churn.

This is a pattern OpenView Partners has documented across high-performing SaaS companies: product portfolio rationalization often improves retention metrics more than product expansion.

1% from Aligning the Data Story with the Growth Story

The final lever was about how churn was presented, not how it was measured.

In the company’s most recent acquisition, approximately 75 customers had provided notification that they would cancel their subscription due to the acquisition, but hadn’t formally churned yet. These were known future churners — M&A-related attrition, not organic business failure.

When building the sell-side data cube, we tagged and fenced off these customers to show their impact separately. This accomplished two things:

  1. It removed ~1% of known M&A-related churn from the organic NRR number
  2. It highlighted the company’s sophistication in handling the complexities of acquiring and integrating bolt-on acquisitions

The result: the organic growth story was cleaner and more defensible — exactly what a buy-side diligence team would want to see.

The Impact: From 101% to 105%

The ending NRR waterfall told a different story: -3% churn, -1% product churn, -1% downsell, +5% upsell, +4% cross-sell = 105% ending NRR.

Three outcomes that mattered at exit:

  • Increased valuation multiple. Showing 5% organic growth put the company in a different category of comparable companies. NRR above 105% changes the conversation from “stable business” to “compounding business” — and the multiple reflects it.
  • Prepared for diligence. The CFO went into sell-side diligence with a clear, confident growth narrative rather than a defensive, explanation-laden position. Every number had a decomposition. Every movement had a driver.
  • Operational alignment. The data team learned to work with finance to drive strategic impact — a capability that transferred to the acquirer and added value beyond the transaction itself.

Why This Matters for Your Exit

Every PE-backed SaaS company approaching exit faces the same question: can you prove that your growth is durable? An ARR Snowball that decomposes NRR into its constituent movements — and connects those movements to operational decisions — is the difference between a story and a proof.

The moves that took this company from 101% to 105% weren’t complex. They were operational: price discipline, product rationalization, and data story alignment. But they required the right analytical foundation to identify, prioritize, and measure.

Every move here was done with the client's team, not handed over as a report — which is why the capability transferred to the acquirer.
