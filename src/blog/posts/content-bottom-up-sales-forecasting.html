Bottom-up sales forecasting builds the landing number from booked revenue and open pipeline at the account, product and rep, then reconciles it to Finance. Pacer AI is that agent for CROs, VPs of Sales, RevOps and CFOs. The point is to make plan with confidence. Real-time forecasting is second: it is how current the landing number stays, not a different kind of forecast.

What is bottom-up sales forecasting?

Start with what is already booked. Add the open pipeline, weighted by the stage rules you are willing to defend. Cut it by the motions that make the number: new logo, upsell, cross-sell, and the contraction and churn you still have to absorb. The result is a landing number for the month, the quarter and the year. A top-down forecast starts with the target and shades it. A bottom-up forecast starts with the deals.

The plan is the intention. The forecast is the current read of that intention. They share definitions. If the forecast’s new-logo line is not the same new-logo line as the plan, you cannot say whether you are ahead.

How is the landing number built?

Four inputs, and none of them is a gut feel written down after the meeting.

  1. Booked ARR on Finance’s definition, the same baseline as the path to plan.
  2. Open pipeline at account, product and rep, with a close period.
  3. Stage weights that are written down. A 50% weight means half of the dollars at that stage have closed in the history you trust, not that the rep is hopeful.
  4. The give-back. Churn and downsell you can already see, so the landing number is not gross hope.

The forecast landing on the homepage chart is $117.6M: booked ARR plus weighted open pipeline, still short of the $120M target. These figures are illustrative.

What does “reconciled to Finance” require?

The CRM records a booking when a deal closes. Finance records recurring revenue as it is recognized, net of contraction, churn and timing. Both can be correct and still not match. The forecast has to sit on the bridge between them, at account and product, so pipeline ties to reported ARR. That bridge is the ARR waterfall. A forecast that never ties out will be rewritten by Finance the week before the board meeting.

Who asks which question?

The CRO asks where the year lands. The VP of Sales asks which reps and which deals move the landing number this month. RevOps asks whether the stage weights still match how deals actually close. The CFO asks whether the landing number uses the same ARR as the books. If those four questions return four numbers, you do not have a forecast. You have four spreadsheets.

Quota is the rep’s share of the plan, not of the forecast. Keep them apart. Plan to quota is the assignment. This page is the current read.

Where does real-time fit?

Once the landing number is built from deals, it should update when a stage changes, a deal closes, or a renewal is lost. That update is the real-time forecast. It is the same bottom-up number, kept current. It is not a second model with a second definition.

SaaS Capital documents how private companies actually retain revenue, which is the give-back in any landing number. Bessemer’s Atlas and OpenView are the board context for growth and retention. SaaStr is where operators argue about pipeline math in public. None of them know your stage weights. You should.

What do written stage weights look like?

Weights are a table, reviewed on a cadence, not a slider in the meeting. An illustrative table, not a recommendation for your cycle:

StageWeightWhat the weight claims
Qualified10%One in ten of these dollars has closed, on the history you trust
Evaluation30%The buyer is in a process you can name
Proposal50%Half of proposal-stage dollars have closed
Commit80%The exceptions are listed, not waved through
Closed100%It is booked, on Finance’s definition

If your commit stage closes at 40%, the table is wrong and the landing number is fiction. Change the weight or change the stage definition. Do not keep an 80% weight because the slide looks better. Reps should see the weight that will be applied to their deals before they call a deal commit. A surprise haircut in the forecast meeting is how the CRM stops being the system of record.

Separate new logo from expansion. A renewal at proposal is not the same object as a new logo at proposal, and they should not share a weight unless the history says they close alike. Churn risk is not a negative weight hidden inside a stage. It is a line in the bridge, with an account name, so the landing number can fall when a renewal is in danger and the new-logo pipe is healthy.

Revisit the table when the motion changes: a new segment, a new product, a cycle that got longer. Until then, leave it. A forecast whose weights move every week cannot be compared with last week, which defeats the reason to have it. The real-time update moves the deals. It does not move the weights.

Inspect the landing number in three cuts every time you publish it: by motion, by segment, and by the ten largest deals in the weight. A healthy total with a hollow new-logo line is a different problem from a healthy total with one deal at commit that is half the gap. The ten-deal list is short enough to review by name. If a name on it cannot be explained by the owner, take it out of the weight until it can. That subtraction is the forecast getting more accurate, not less ambitious.

Frequently asked questions

What is bottom-up sales forecasting?

A landing number built from booked revenue and weighted open pipeline at the account, product and rep, reconciled to Finance’s ARR.

How is it different from the plan?

The plan is what you intended to produce. The forecast is what the book and the pipe say you will produce. They use the same motions and the same ARR definition.

Does the forecast replace the path to plan?

No. The path compares bookings with the prorated plan and ignores unclosed pipe. The forecast adds the pipe. You want both, or a fat pipeline will hide a thin book.
